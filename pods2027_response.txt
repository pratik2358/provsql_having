A. WHY THE ANNOTATION OF AN AGGREGATE COMPARISON MUST BE DECOMPOSED
(Reviewer 1, last two comments)

Reviewer 1 writes that W always equals U since there is no incomplete
information, and that after grouping we know which aggregate values
satisfy the selection. We think this is mistaken, and as it is the heart
of the paper we set out why.

We have to decompose an aggregate comparison, for a reason that has
nothing to do with uncertainty: whether COUNT(*) >= 2 holds on a group is
not a function of the annotations of that group taken as a whole. Those
annotations can be combined by ⊕ and ⊗ into a single element of K, but no
such element records how many occurrences are being counted, so there is
nothing to apply the comparison to unless the group is split first.
Definition 10 performs that split, and ann_U(W) weights each part by
“these occurrences contribute (⊗) and the others do not (⊖)”, the ⊖ being
what makes distinct parts incompatible so that the ⊕ does not count the
same situation twice.

Which decomposition is the right one is settled in an arbitrary
m-semiring, without reference to worlds. A query with COUNT(*) ★ C can
also be written with self-joins and no aggregation at all, and the
provenance of that query is fixed by [18, 15] (and [31] for duplicate
elimination). On a group of three occurrences annotated x1, x2, x3, with
the predicate COUNT(*) >= 2, the self-join query has provenance

    (x1 ⊗ x2) ⊕ (x1 ⊗ x3) ⊕ (x2 ⊗ x3),

and Theorem 13(i) shows our definition returns exactly this, in every
commutative absorptive m-semiring where ⊗ distributes over ⊖. The reading
W = U instead gives x1 ⊗ x2 ⊗ x3, a different element of K: in the
tropical semiring over ℕ, which satisfies both hypotheses, the first is
min(x1+x2, x1+x3, x2+x3) and the second x1+x2+x3, so the two disagree on
the cheapest way of witnessing the answer. Parts (ii) and (iii) of the
theorem show that both hypotheses on the semiring are needed for our
definition and the self-join rewriting to agree at all.

Our name “possible world” is borrowed from the Boolean reading of
annotations and may be misleading outside it: in ℕ an annotation is a
multiplicity and in the tropical semiring a cost, and only in B and B[X]
do the W's denote sub-instances. That special case is nonetheless the one
that yields Proposition 17: there the two elements above are the majority
function and x1 ∧ x2 ∧ x3, and the latter is false on the sub-instance
{x1, x2}, where the group has two occurrences and does satisfy the
predicate. This is why our definition, and not the W = U reading, gives
correct probabilistic evaluation.

On the second remark: after grouping we do not know which aggregate
values satisfy the selection, because the aggregate value of a group is
not a value of V but a formal object of V_K, determined only once one
knows which occurrences of the group contribute. This is exactly why [6]
introduced an uninterpreted comparison operator rather than evaluating
the comparison. Our contribution is to give that operator a value in K.


B. THE “JOIN-BASED SEMANTICS” IS NOT A COMPETING PROPOSAL (Reviewer 2, M6
and Theorem 13)

Our terminology misled Reviewer 2, and we apologise for it. There is no
second semantics of HAVING in the paper. The queries Q2 of Theorem 13
contain no aggregation and no HAVING at all: they are ordinary queries of
the extended relational algebra of Section 2 (self-joins, projection,
duplicate elimination, difference), and their provenance is the
pre-existing semantics of [18, 15, 31] recalled there. Theorem 13 is thus
a soundness check: on COUNT(*) ★ C our semantics returns exactly the
provenance a user obtains today by hand-writing the self-join rewriting
(as the authors of [38] had to do, precisely because no semantics for
HAVING existed, cf. Section 6.2), in every commutative absorptive
m-semiring where ⊗ distributes over ⊖; parts (ii) and (iii) show both
hypotheses are necessary. In the revision we will call it “the self-join
rewriting” and recall its (standard) semantics before the theorem.

Relatedly (Reviewer 2, M5): there is no HAVING operator in the paper
because HAVING is not a new operator. A HAVING clause is a selection σ_ψ
whose predicate ψ compares aggregate values. The syntax of queries is
that of [31], unchanged, and our contribution modifies exactly one rule
of its semantics, that of σ (Definition 11, second case). We should have
said this explicitly.


REVIEWER 1

1. Terminal aggregation. It means that γ may occur only as the outermost
operator of a query, so no selection, join or further grouping may be
applied to an aggregate value; in particular HAVING is not expressible.
The restriction is a severe one in practice, since HAVING is an ordinary
part of SQL: two of the twenty-two queries of TPC-H, Q11 and Q18, use it.
The query family of our experiments illustrates the same point: “return
the images containing at least k objects of class c” is not expressible
with terminal aggregation, which is why [38], on the same probabilistic
image database, had to encode it as an iterated self-join. We will recall
this explicitly in Section 2.

2. Comparison with Amsterdamer, Deutch, Tannen [6]. Concretely: (a) their
aggregates must be commutative monoid operations, since aggregation is
lifted as the semimodule sum, which excludes AVG, ARRAY_AGG/LISTAGG and
the non-commutative aggregates we support (we allow any function on
sequences of values); (b) most importantly, their comparison operator is
left uninterpreted, and no definition is supplied for any combination of
semiring and aggregation monoid. On the example above, with COUNT(*)
modelled by the aggregation monoid (ℕ, +), the aggregate value of the
group is in [6] the semimodule element x1 ⊠ 1 + x2 ⊠ 1 + x3 ⊠ 1, and the
annotation [6] attaches to the result of the comparison is

    δ(x1 ⊕ x2 ⊕ x3) ⊗ [ x1 ⊠ 1 + x2 ⊠ 1 + x3 ⊠ 1  ≥  ι(2) ]

(⊠ is the tensor of an annotation with a value, + the semimodule sum, ⊕
and ⊗ the semiring operations, δ the operator of [6] marking the
existence of the group, and ι(2) the constant 2 viewed as a semimodule
element). The bracket is a fresh indeterminate of an extended polynomial
semiring. It receives a value only under a homomorphism into a semiring
where the aggregate becomes a concrete value, as in their ℕ[X] → ℕ
example; within K itself it stays symbolic, so for K = B[X] the
annotation remains a polynomial in such indeterminates rather than a
Boolean function. Definition 11 gives that same comparison the value (x1
∧ x2) ∨ (x1 ∧ x3) ∨ (x2 ∧ x3). That is what makes probability evaluation
(Proposition 17) and an implementation possible, and [29], which follows
[6], indeed stops at storing such expressions as strings. We will add
this comparison with the example.

3. Multiset over V_K^k × K rather than a partial function V_K^k → K. The
reviewer is right that this is not the usual formulation, and we should
have justified it. In the usual one a K-relation is a function on tuple
values, so there is exactly one entry per value and multiplicity, when it
is wanted, is pushed into the annotation, as in ℕ or ℕ[X].

The first reason is aggregation itself, and it has nothing to do with
provenance: an aggregate is computed on the occurrences of a group, not
on its distinct values. On a group where the attribute a takes the value
5 three times, SUM(a) is 15 and COUNT(*) is 3, whereas the function
formulation offers a single entry for the value 5. The multiplicity has
to be recorded somewhere, and [6] records it in the annotation: their
aggregate value is the semimodule sum k1 ⊠ m1 + … + kn ⊠ mn over the
distinct values, in which the annotation acts on the value. This works,
but under a condition they make explicit: K and the aggregation monoid M
must be compatible, i.e. ι: M → K ⊗ M must be injective, and when ⊕ is
idempotent a compatible M is idempotent as well [6, Proposition 3.11];
already in B ⊗ SUM, ι(4) = ι(2) + ι(2) = ι(2). A non-idempotent
aggregate such as SUM or COUNT thus requires a semiring admitting a
homomorphism to ℕ [6, Theorem 3.13]. But absorptive semirings are
idempotent (take b = 1 in a ⊕ (a ⊗ b) = a), and so is B[X], which we
need for probabilistic evaluation: COUNT(*) is exactly a non-idempotent
aggregate over an idempotent semiring, and the annotations cannot carry
the multiplicities for us. We keep them in the data instead, a group
being a sequence of occurrences and annotations being attached to
possible worlds W ⊑ U rather than folded into the aggregate value, so
that idempotence of ⊕ costs nothing: it is W that records how many
occurrences are counted.

The second reason is that a system computing provenance for actual SQL
must follow bag semantics: a projection such as SELECT
a FROM R turns two distinct rows of R agreeing on a into two occurrences
of a single value tuple, carrying different provenance x1 and x2, which
a function into K has to merge into one entry annotated x1 ⊕ x2. HAVING
is what makes the loss observable: on the merged entry the group has a
single member, so COUNT(*) >= 2 gives the semiring zero in B[X], whereas
on the two occurrences it gives x1 ∧ x2. This is why the setting of [31,
Section IV], which we inherit, keeps occurrences distinct, each with its
own provenance identifier. We will spell all of this out.

4. Line 143, human-readable proofs for Table 1. The reviewer is right
that they are not in the appendix: the table is verified in Lean only,
each entry hyperlinked to its machine-checked counterpart. It records,
for 13 m-semirings, the definition of ⊖ together with four properties,
i.e. on the order of sixty individually routine verifications. We will
add readable proofs for the two entries that correct the literature ([15,
Example 4] and [5, Axiom A13]).

5. Line 226, the ★ symbol. We will change it. Two things make it a bad
choice beyond taste: * is the closure operator in the semiring
literature, and the paper itself uses a star in an unrelated sense in the
statement of Theorem 13, where <* denotes < augmented with an
occurrence-based tie-break. We will use θ, the usual metavariable for a
comparison operator in relational algebra, as in θ-joins. The symbol is
not an operator of the language but a metavariable ranging over {<, ≤, =,
≥, >, ≠}, so that a single definition covers all six.

6. Definition 10, “multiset of annotated tuples” vs. K-relation. Yes,
these are the same object; we will use “K-relation” throughout.

7. Definition 10, “sequence of tuples taken with their multiplicity”, and
the order of the products. U is the K-relation of the group linearised
into a sequence, each occurrence contributing as many elements as its
multiplicity, ordered by ≼ on the value component (ties broken
arbitrarily). Multiplicities are used: the ⊗-product and ⊕-sum in
ann_U(W) range over the elements of the sequences W and U∖W, so a tuple
occurring three times contributes three factors, and multiplicities
determine the aggregate value (COUNT(*) counts occurrences). The product
is indeed taken in the order of W, but this is immaterial since we
restrict to commutative m-semirings (Section 2); the order matters only
for agg_{t,f} with a non-commutative f. We will state both points
explicitly.

8. “Value part”. Agreed; we will simply write “the tuple u of the
annotated tuple (u, α)”.

9. Where W comes from. W is universally quantified: Definition 10 defines
the possible worlds of U to be all subsequences W ⊑ U, and Definition 11
sums over all of them. Nothing is left dangling, but we agree that the
intuition (paragraph A above) is missing and must precede the formalism.

10. Ordinary vs. regular, types vs. values, and “is 42 an ordinary
value?”. The inconsistency is ours and we will fix it. There is a single
distinction and it is static: V_K ⊇ V, the values of V are the regular
ones and the values of V_K∖V are the formal aggregate values produced by
γ. A term has non-regular type if and only if it refers to an output
column m+l of a grouping operator; constants, group-key attributes and
arithmetic over them are regular. So 42 is a regular value, the sum of
three regular values is regular, and the aggregate produced by γ is
non-regular even though in each individual world it evaluates to a
number, precisely because it is not determined by the K-instance.
Definition 11 phrases the test in terms of the values reached, which is
the same criterion but is stated inconsistently with the text preceding
it; we will phrase it as type-based in both places and drop “ordinary” in
favour of “regular”/“non-regular”.

11. γ in Definition 11. γ is not introduced by Definition 10, and it is
not our notation: it is the standard operator for extending the
relational algebra with grouping and aggregation, due to Libkin [25],
transposed to annotated relations by [31] and recalled in our Section 2.
Definition 10 only fixes the ingredients γ needs, namely the grouping
indices and the order ≼. We will give the definition of
γ^≼_{i1,…,im}[t1:f1,…,tn:fn] explicitly.

12. “s is the aggregate column m+l”, but s was announced to be a term. s
ranges over the two sides {t, t'} of the comparison and both are terms;
the phrase abbreviates “s is the positional term #(m+l)”. We will write
it that way.

13. Example 12: v, u1, u2, u3, u. These are three different objects, and
the example never says so. v is the group key; u1, u2, u3 are the
pre-aggregation occurrences forming the group, i.e. the elements of U,
each with its annotation xi; and u = (v, b1, …, bn) is the tuple output
by γ, on which the predicate is evaluated. The omission is u, which
appears only inside the final expression, with no sentence introducing
it. We will fix this.

l417, extended relational algebra query with HAVING. A query of RA_k

l419, Pr(I ⊨ Q). This is the standard probability of a Boolean query over

is true. Their equality is the correctness of intensional probabilistic
evaluation and is not automatic: it is exactly the characteristic
property of paragraph A, and what has to be established for the new σ
rule. The proof is in the appendix. We will give the definition in the
body.

correctness means here. We will make this link explicit.

L89, “statements presupposing a Lean implementation of HAVING”. The Lean

Theorem 13, notation and the class of queries. Agreed. Q_1^{★C} and
Q_2^{★C} are families parameterised by an arbitrary arity-2 query q, by C
and by ★. Arity 2 is not a restriction on the pre-aggregation query but
what the self-join rewriting requires: the second column carries the
tie-break <* enforcing distinct matches under multiset semantics. We will

reduction, instead of starting from the arity-2 form.

Proposition 21, SUM(a) ≤ / < / = c in absorptive m-semirings. The reason
those cases are absent is not absorptivity.

 - For COUNT(*) ≤ k, < k, = k with k fixed, the valid worlds are exactly
   those of size at most k, so there are only O(N^k) of them in total.
   This is why Case 4 of the proof needs no assumption on the semiring at
   all: tractability there comes from the bound on the size of the
   worlds, not from absorptivity.
 - Absorptivity helps only for upward-closed families (the ≥ and >
   cases), where it lets us keep only the minimal valid worlds. For ≤, <
   and = the family is not upward-closed, so absorptivity buys nothing,
   and without a further hypothesis the valid worlds are exponentially
   many: if all values of a are 0, every non-empty world is valid for
   SUM(a) ≤ c.
 - However, under the very hypothesis we already make in Case 3 (every
   value a of the attribute satisfies c/a ≤ k, with c > 0, so a ≥ c/k),
   any world whose sum is ≤ c, < c or = c has at most k occurrences, and
   the counting argument of Case 4 applies verbatim. These three cases
   are therefore in PTIME as well, and in arbitrary m-semirings rather
   than only absorptive ones. No new algorithm is required: ValidSum
   already covers the six comparisons, and for ≤, < and = its dynamic
   programme is bounded by the constant c, so it enumerates exactly the
   polynomially many valid worlds. The absorptivity-based pruning, both
   in the proof and in our implementation, applies only to the ≥ and >
   cases, which is the asymmetry the reviewer noticed. We will add these
   cases to the proposition.

L193, D(R). D is the database schema; in the unnamed perspective it maps
each relation name to its arity, and D(R) is the arity of R. We will
clarify.

L257, an example. Agreed. Line 257 is the end of Definition 10, and the
first illustration of the machinery, Example 12, comes only after

PRESENTATION

We accept the criticism of Reviewers 1 and 2 on the presentation from
Section 3 onwards. We promise a good many additions above, so we should
say how they fit in the page limit.

Most of them cost no space at all, being local rewordings: the symbol and
terminology changes (★, “ordinary” against “regular”, “value part”,
“multiset of annotated tuples”, the U notation, the section title), the
corrected sentences on rings and on l246, and the missing sentence
introducing u in Example 12. The one real addition to the body is what
both reviewers ask for first, and what we think would have prevented most
of the rest: motivation before the formalism at the start of Section 3,
together with the observation of paragraph B that we modify exactly one
rule of an existing semantics. That is a paragraph, not a section.

The bulky item is the language we inherit: its syntax, its terms and
predicates, and the rules of [31] we leave unchanged. We will recall it
self-contained in the appendix and keep in the body only the rule we
modify. This is not the situation Reviewer 2 objects to in M7: Algorithms
1 and 2 are a contribution of ours, whereas this is background recalled
from prior work. Should the committee prefer either in the body, the room
can come from the implementation-details paragraph of Section 6.1 and
from part of Section 7.
