REVIEWER 1

Q1/W1. The computation is correct and the objection is justified; we found the same defect after submission. The rule of Section 3 (L312–317) reads ∧ and ∨ atom by atom, so the product of two sums picks up terms from different worlds: here 0.125 = ann({1}) ⊗ ann({1,2}). Such terms vanish only if a ⊗ (1 ⊖ a) = 0 for all a, which fails in Viterbi. The fix follows Definition 10 itself: a predicate over one group takes a single ⊕-sum, over the worlds where the whole predicate holds. In the example no world qualifies, and the annotation is 0. The two readings coincide when ⊗ is also idempotent, as in B[X] (proved in Lean), so Boolean provenance and Proposition 14 are unaffected. So are all results on a single comparison (Theorems 12 and 16, Propositions 17 and 19) and the experiments. Theorem 29(ii) and the remark at L1335–1337 do depend on the old rule: their compound case will be restricted to a single comparison. The Lean formalization and the implementation have already been corrected, and the final version of the paper will be updated accordingly.

Q2/W2 (and R3, W2). Yes, the benchmark is one query family without joins: WHERE obj = c GROUP BY img HAVING COUNT(*) θ k. Each detected object is an independent probabilistic fact, and the provenance of an image is the Boolean function stating that at least (or at most) k of its class-c detections are correct; its probability is the probability that the image is an answer. The section is not meant as a comprehensive benchmark of a system: its purpose is to show that the semantics is implementable and can bring a performance gain. This family serves that purpose, as it is the query that [42] had to write as a k-fold self-join, no semantics for HAVING being available. The rewriting is faster for small k, then its cost grows with the number of joins, up to about 100 times ours at k = 15, while ours stays between 0.2 and 0.6 s. R3's observations are accurate, and Section 6 states the bound on group size. The general algorithmic claims (SUM, other aggregates, other semirings) rest on the proofs of Section 5, not on these experiments.

Comments. L364: “this mismatch” refers to items (ii) and (iii): in some m-semirings, the semantics and the self-join rewriting disagree. Proposition 17: agreed, the sketch is terse; the full proof is in Appendix D. Corrections: the contribution we intend is the compilation of these m-semirings with machine-checked proofs, not the corrections as such, which are a by-product. The final version will not list them among the contributions, and will keep them in Section 2 and Appendix A.1.

REVIEWER 2

Lean–paper correspondence. A precision on the premise: the paper, including all its proofs, is human-written, and so is the formalization's foundation. The first part of the Lean development was written by hand, and the proofs of later results were generated with an LLM. Every proof being checked by the Lean kernel, trust rests on the kernel and on the statements; the definitions and theorem statements were written or reviewed by us and compared by hand with those of the paper. Each hyperlink leads to the formal statement and its source, so that any reader can repeat this comparison, and the anonymous sources build can be compiled to check that the proofs are indeed verified by the kernel, without any extra axioms needed. L957–960 states where the formalized language is narrower than Definition 10. The comparison being manual, it can miss things: R3's W3 is a discrepancy between Definition 10 and the formalized Definition 20. Similarly, Lean checks proofs, not whether a definition is the right one: the formalization faithfully proved results about the rule criticized in R1's W1.

Theorem 12. Indeed it is not a dichotomy: (i) covers all K in its class, all q, θ, and C, and all instances, while (ii) and (iii) each exhibit one K and one instance, with q a base relation and one comparison, showing that neither hypothesis can simply be dropped. For (iii), more holds: for every idempotent, distributive, non-absorptive K and q a base relation, Q^{≥1} disagrees on some instance. If x = 1 ⊕ b ≠ 1, the group (x, x) gives x² against x, and x² = x would yield x(x ⊖ 1) = x² ⊖ x = 0, which is impossible as x ⊖ 1 ≠ 0 and x ≥ 1. So among idempotent distributive m-semirings, agreement for all q, θ, C, and instances holds exactly for absorptive ones. For (ii), Q^{≥C} agrees in every absorptive K (Theorem 29(i)), so only = and ≤ can disagree.

W1. On the relation to earlier work: [6] introduces comparisons as formal expressions, for commutative monoid aggregates; they resolve to a value only when the semimodule is isomorphic to the aggregation monoid, so not in B[X]. [34] follows [6] and records such expressions without evaluating them. New here is a value in K for any comparison, in any commutative m-semiring, for any aggregate on sequences, which is what makes evaluation and probability computation possible. As the appendix was only consulted cursorily, we point to the proof of Theorem 12 (Appendix C.1, about 10 pages), Theorem 29, and Proposition 15. Identifying the hypotheses of Theorem 12 took considerable effort: idempotence, the natural candidate, does not suffice (iii); absorptivity is needed at a single step of the recurrence (L381–383); and distributivity only for = and ≤, not for ≥ (Theorem 29(i)).

Specific points: δ(β) marks that a group exists (L203–204), the factor of L137, and δ-absorption allows dropping it next to a possible-world sum. The order in γ is needed only for non-commutative aggregates (L189–192). Π and the full syntax are in Appendix A.3. L175: N* denotes the positive integers as positions start at index 1, as in SQL's “GROUP BY 1”. W3. Definition 10 proper covers a single comparison; the paragraph that follows extends the predicate provenance to Boolean combinations, but the paper does not state separately what becomes of α there. Read through the second case of the selection rule, your example gives 1, which is not intended. Definition 20 is the intended semantics: it drops the group-existence factor only when ψ entails that the group exists, which gives δ(x) ⊗ (1 ⊕ 0) = x on your example. The results rest on Definition 20: Proposition 14 is interpreted under it, and its proof and that of Proposition 13 treat mixed regular atoms explicitly (“a regular atom entails nothing”), as does Proposition 22; the Lean formalization (GenPred.entailsExistence) and the implementation follow it.

W1. Agreed: “Different Differences in Semirings” should have been discussed, and the final version will cite it. We do not claim these monus definitions as new, but we claim their compilation in one place (Table 1), all machine-checked. Appendix A.1 already credits the earlier observation of the tropical semiring flaw in [17, Example 4] to [4]; it will also credit footnote 6 of this paper. Interesingly, the “Different Differences” paper also illustrates why our compilation matters: it states that (A5), the distributivity of ⊗ over ⊖, holds in N[X] (bottom of page 10:7, also used in footnote 10), whereas (x ⊖ 1)(x + 1) = x² + x and (x² + x) ⊖ (x + 1) = x², as we show in Appendix A.1 and in our Lean formalization. Such statements are easy to get wrong, even in careful work by experts, hence Table 1 and its machine-checked proofs.
